01

01How scent works

Every perfume is a pyramid.

Here's the thing: a perfume isn't one smell. It's three, arriving in shifts. Light, fast molecules hit your nose first and leave first. Heavy ones take their time and stay all day. Perfumers stack them into a pyramid — and once you see it, every bottle you've ever smelled suddenly makes sense.

Layer 1 of 3 · the opening

Top notes

Lasts ~30 min

The opening line. Small, zippy molecules that evaporate fast — they're the first thing you smell after a spritz, and the reason perfumes smell so good in the shop. Bright, sharp, gone quickly.

  • Bergamot
  • Lemon zest
  • Grapefruit
  • Sweet orange
  • Peppermint
  • Lavender
  • Pink pepper
  • Eucalyptus

In your formula: ~30%

Fragrance families

Perfumes are grouped into families by their overall mood. Pick one to see what it smells like.

Flowers, front and centre — from dewy and green to heady and powdery. The biggest family in perfumery, and the easiest to start with.

  • Rose + geranium
  • Jasmine + neroli
  • Lavender + honeyed vanilla
02

02Your first batch · today

Make something today.

No waiting, no special shop. This is the warm-oil roll-on: you gently heat kitchen botanicals in oil so their scent moves out of the plant and into the oil — in an afternoon instead of a month. It's soft, sits close to the skin, and is honestly lovely.

The kit

What you need

  • 90 ml (6 tbsp) oilJojoba or fractionated (liquid) coconut oil. Regular coconut oil works too, but it turns solid below 24°C / 76°F.
  • 2–3 scent ingredients, dryTry: 1 vanilla bean, split · peel of 1 orange or lemon · 1 tbsp dried lavender.
  • A 10 ml glass roll-on bottlePlus a tiny funnel. Amber or cobalt glass keeps light out.
  • A heatproof jar & a saucepanYou'll make a gentle water bath.
  • A fine strainer + a coffee filterFor a clean, clear finish.

25% scent · 75% oil — by volume, right in the 20–30% sweet spot.

About vanilla extract It's water and alcohol, so it won't mix with oil. Use a bean here — save the extract for the alcohol method in Chapter 03.

  1. 1

    10 min

    Dry anything fresh.

    Peel or zest your citrus — just the coloured part — and let it dry on a plate for a few hours, or 10–15 minutes in a very low oven (90°C / 200°F). Herbs and petals too. Bone dry is the goal.

    Why this worksDry botanicals give their scent to oil. Wet ones give it water — and water in oil is how mould starts.

  2. 2

    2 min

    Measure: 1 part scent, 3 parts oil.

    Put 2 tbsp (30 ml) of loosely packed botanicals in your clean, dry jar. Pour 6 tbsp (90 ml) of oil over them. Everything should be fully covered — push floaters down with a clean spoon.

    Why this worksAbout 25% botanicals is the sweet spot: plenty of plant to scent the oil, but little enough that nothing pokes out above the surface and spoils.

  3. 3

    2 hrs

    Warm it low and slow.

    Stand the open jar in a saucepan with 5 cm of water and set it on the lowest heat. You want warm, not frying: roughly 50–60°C (120–140°F) — too hot to hold the jar for long, nowhere near simmering. Stir every 20 minutes and top up the water as it evaporates.

    Why this worksGentle heat speeds up what a cold infusion does in weeks. Too hot and you cook off the delicate top notes, so keep it lazy.

  4. 4

    15 min

    Strain it twice.

    Let it cool until just warm. Pour it through a fine strainer, pressing the botanicals to get the last drops, then through a coffee filter. Want it stronger? Put fresh botanicals in the same oil and repeat step 3.

    Why this worksAny plant bits left behind will spoil. A double strain is the difference between a roll-on that lasts months and one that lasts days.

  5. 5

    5 min

    Bottle, label, wear.

    Funnel it into the roll-on. Write the date and ingredients on a label. Patch test first: a dab on your inner forearm, then wait 24 hours. All clear? Roll it onto wrists, neck and behind the ears. Keep it cool and dark, use within 3–6 months, and toss it if it smells off.

    Why this worksPulse points are warm, and warmth lifts scent off your skin. Oil holds it close, so expect a soft, personal scent rather than a cloud.

Step 6 — there isn't one

You just made perfume. Now let's go deeper.

It'll be softer than store-bought — that's normal. Commercial perfume is made from concentrated aroma materials; you used a kitchen. The next chapters close the gap.

03Three ways to make it

Alcohol, oil, or water.

Each method pulls scent out of plants with a different liquid. Choose by what you want to wear — and how long you're willing to wait.

MakesSpray perfume
Steep1–6 weeks
Then age4–6 weeks
On skin1–3 hrs

Ingredients

  • A clean glass jar with a tight lid (about 250 ml)
  • The highest-proof alcohol you can get: Everclear 190 (95%) where it's legal, otherwise 100-proof vodka (50%). Perfumer's alcohol, sold online, is the pro option.
  • Botanicals: petals, citrus peel, herbs, whole spices — dried works best
  • Optional: a split vanilla bean or a little benzoin resin as a fixative
  • Coffee filters and a glass spray bottle

Steps

  1. Bruise herbs and peel lightly between your fingers, then fill the jar about halfway, loosely packed.
  2. Cover completely with alcohol, leaving 2 cm of space at the top. Seal it and write the date on the lid.
  3. Keep it somewhere dark at room temperature. Shake it once a day.
  4. Smell it every few days. Delicate petals are usually done in 1–2 weeks; peels, spices and woods take 3–6.
  5. Want it stronger? Strain out the spent botanicals, add fresh ones to the same alcohol, and repeat — up to three rounds.
  6. Strain through a coffee filter into your spray bottle. Then age it 4–6 more weeks in the dark before you judge it.

Honest caveats

Strength
Much lighter than commercial perfume. Think fresh cologne, not a statement scent.
Longevity
1–3 hours on skin, noticeably longer on a scarf.
Spoilage
Keeps a year or more if the alcohol is high-proof. Low-proof vodka carries more water, which can turn it cloudy and weak.
Fire
High-proof alcohol is flammable. No flames nearby — and never, ever use rubbing alcohol.
MakesDab-on perfume oil
Steep4–6 weeks
Then ageNot needed
On skin3–6 hrs

Ingredients

  • Liquid carrier oil: fractionated coconut, jojoba, or sweet almond (skip almond if nut allergies are a concern)
  • Dried botanicals only — the same petals, peels, herbs and spices as the vodka method
  • A clean, completely dry glass jar
  • Cheesecloth or coffee filters
  • Roll-on bottles or a small dab jar

Steps

  1. Fill the jar about a third of the way with dried botanicals.
  2. Pour in oil until the botanicals are covered by at least 2–3 cm. Stir with a clean, dry spoon to release air bubbles.
  3. Seal, label with the date, and keep it somewhere warm but out of direct sun.
  4. Turn the jar gently every day or two for 4–6 weeks.
  5. Strain through cheesecloth, squeezing hard, then filter again. For more strength, repeat with fresh botanicals.
  6. Bottle it. It's ready to wear straight away and will soften a little over the first week.

Honest caveats

Strength
Soft and close. People near you won't smell much — that's the charm.
Longevity
Usually outlasts the vodka version on skin, because oil evaporates slowly.
Spoilage
Oils go rancid eventually (you'll smell crayons or stale nuts). Most last 6–12 months; jojoba lasts longest. Any moisture means mould risk.
Stains
Oil can mark silk and pale fabric. Apply to skin, not clothes.
MakesLight body mist
Simmer~2 hours
Then ageNot needed
Keeps1–2 weeks, chilled

Ingredients

  • About 2 cups of fragrant, unsprayed petals — rose, lavender, orange blossom
  • Distilled water (tap-water minerals dull the scent and speed up spoilage)
  • A stainless steel or enamel pot with a lid
  • A strainer, a spray bottle, and room in the fridge

Steps

  1. Rinse the petals gently, put them in the pot, and add just enough distilled water to cover.
  2. Lid on. Bring it to just under a simmer — steaming, tiny bubbles, never a rolling boil.
  3. Hold it there for up to 2 hours, until the petals have lost most of their colour. Top up water if it gets low.
  4. Turn off the heat and let it cool completely with the lid on — the lid keeps the scent in.
  5. Strain into a clean spray bottle, label it, and refrigerate straight away.

Upgrade Put a small bowl on an upturned ramekin in the middle of the pot, flip the lid upside down and pile ice on it. Steam condenses on the cold lid and drips into the bowl — a real kitchen distillation, and a stronger floral water.

Honest caveats

Strength
The lightest of the three. A fresh, fleeting mist.
Longevity
Minutes to an hour on skin. Lovely on pillows and linen.
Spoilage
No preservative means bacteria grow fast. Keep it in the fridge, use within 1–2 weeks, and toss it at the first sour smell or cloudiness.
Selling
Don't. Unpreserved water products aren't safe to sell.

04The kitchen apothecary

You already own half a lab.

What's in your cupboard, what each thing does to a perfume, and how to use it without wasting it.

Top

Citrus peel & zest

Bright, juicy top notes — the “hello” of your perfume.

Zest only the coloured part; the white pith is bitter. Dry it before using it in oil. Brilliant in alcohol. Heads up: citrus on skin plus strong sun can burn (see Chapter 08).

Ready-made fixative

Base · Fixative

Real vanilla extract

Warm, sweet, creamy — and it slows the whole blend's evaporation, so everything lasts longer.

Buy pure extract, not “imitation.” It goes straight into alcohol blends. For oil, use a split vanilla bean instead — extract won't mix with oil.

Top · Heart

Herbs

Rosemary · mint · thyme · lavender

Green, clean, aromatic freshness.

Dry them before oil infusions. Roll leaves between your palms to wake them up. Lavender is the most perfume-ready herb you own.

Heart · Base

Spices

Cinnamon · clove · cardamom · coffee beans

Warmth and depth — the cosy heart of amber scents.

Use whole and lightly cracked; ground spice turns blends muddy. Go easy — cinnamon and clove can irritate skin. One stick or a few cloves per jar is plenty.

Carrier

Coconut oil

The neutral base that holds everything else.

For roll-ons, buy fractionated (liquid) coconut oil. Regular coconut oil goes solid below 24°C / 76°F and smells of coconut.

Heart

Rose petals

The classic heart note — soft, romantic, a little honeyed.

Use fragrant, unsprayed garden roses; shop-bought florist roses are often treated with pesticides. Old-fashioned and deep red varieties smell strongest. Best in alcohol or as floral water.

✕

The short list

Never use these.

  • Rubbing alcohol (isopropyl)It's toxic, your skin absorbs it, and it dries and irritates. Perfume uses ethanol — the drinkable kind — only.
  • Candle, soap or craft fragrance oilsUnless they're specifically labelled skin-safe, they're made for wax, not bodies — and often contain materials never tested for skin.
  • Anything mouldy, slimy or sour-smellingBotanicals or finished batches. You can't strain mould out. Bin it and start again.

Perfume is just math.

Every bottle is two things: fragrance concentrate (your oils or tinctures) and a carrier (alcohol for a spray, oil for a roll-on). Strength is simply the percentage that's concentrate. Pick a bottle, pick a strength, and the numbers do the rest.

07

07The longevity playbook

Make it last all day.

The number-one complaint about homemade perfume: “It's gone in an hour.” That's fixable — mostly. Five laws, in order.

Start your formula at the bottom, not the top. Base notes — vanilla, sandalwood, cedar, amber, musk, patchouli — are big, slow molecules that last 8–12 hours or more. Get your 20% base right first, then build the heart and top on it. A blend with no base is a blend that leaves.

  • Vanilla
  • Sandalwood
  • Cedar
  • Amber
  • Musk
  • Patchouli

A fixative is a heavy, slightly sticky material that slows down the evaporation of everything around it — like a paperweight on your top notes. The two easiest to find are vanilla (a bean, or pure extract in alcohol blends) and benzoin resin, a warm, vanilla-ish tree resin sold as a powder or tincture. A little goes a long way: start small and add more in your next batch.

Concentration is the whole difference between a cologne and a parfum. Cologne is 2–4% concentrate and fades in a couple of hours; parfum is 20–30% and can carry you through the day. If yours vanishes, raise the percentage in the calculator — staying within the skin limits of the materials you use.

Cologne 2–4%Parfum 20–30%

This is the big one. Freshly mixed alcohol perfume smells sharp, thin and boozy, and it falls apart on skin. Over 4–6 weeks in a dark cupboard, the ingredients blend and mellow — perfumers call it maturation. Skipping this step is why so much homemade perfume vanishes in an hour. Mark the ready date on the bottle and don't peek early.

Oil evaporates more slowly than alcohol, so perfume oils sit on skin longer. Better still, layer them: unscented moisturiser first (dry skin loses scent fast), then your oil, then a spritz of spray on top. And use fabric and hair — they hold scent for 24 hours or more. Mist hair from a distance, and test fabric somewhere hidden first; oil can mark it.

  1. Moisturiser
  2. Perfume oil
  3. Spray
  4. Scarf & hair

Honest expectations · a 24-hour wear

Hours 1–4It projects.

People near you notice.

Hours 4–12A skin scent.

You smell it when you lean in.

Hours 12–24A whisper.

Mostly on your scarf.

That's as 24-hour as perfume gets. Homemade blends sit at the shorter end of every range — commercial perfumes use concentrated aroma materials a kitchen can't match. Aim for great, not eternal.

08

08Safety

Careful, not scared.

Perfume-making is safe when you respect six rules. None of them are hard. All of them matter.

01

Patch test everything.

Dab a little of the finished blend on your inner forearm, cover it with a plaster, and wait 24 hours. Redness, itching or bumps? That blend isn't for your skin. Do it for every new formula, not just the first one.

  1. 01Dab a coin-sized spot
  2. 02Cover it
  3. 03Wait 24 hours
  4. 04Check, then decide
24h
02

Citrus + sun = burns.

Some citrus oils — cold-pressed bergamot, lime and lemon especially — react with UV light and can cause blistering and dark marks. It's called phototoxicity. Don't wear citrus-heavy blends on skin that'll see strong sun; wear them on clothes instead, or at night.

03

Nothing goes on undiluted.

Essential oils, absolutes and resins are concentrated. Neat on skin, they can burn — or sensitise you, meaning you react to that ingredient for good. Always dilute into a carrier first.

04

Alcohol is flammable.

High-proof alcohol catches fire easily and its vapour travels. No open flames, gas burners or candles nearby. Ventilate the room, never heat alcohol on the stove, and store it away from children and pets.

05

Water spoils.

Floral waters and anything containing water have no preservative. Keep them in the fridge, use them within 1–2 weeks, and toss them at the first sour smell, cloudiness or floating bits.

06

Label every batch.

Date, ingredients, amounts, method. If something irritates your skin, the label tells you what did it. If something is perfect, it tells you how to make it again.

When to stop and see a doctor: a rash that spreads or won't fade, any swelling, or trouble breathing. Trouble breathing is an emergency — call emergency services.

09Selling it

From hobby to business.

Here's the honest version. People sell perfume from their kitchens every day — but the moment money changes hands, you're a cosmetics manufacturer. This is a US-focused map of what that means. It's guidance, not legal advice: rules change, so check the current official source for every step.

  1. 01

    It's a cosmetic.

    In the US, perfume is regulated as a cosmetic by the FDA. The FDA doesn't pre-approve cosmetics before they go on sale — which sounds easy until you realise what it means: you are responsible for making sure your product is safe and properly labelled.

    Verify before selling · FDA.gov
  2. 02

    Know the MoCRA rules.

    The Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 (MoCRA) added duties for cosmetics makers: registering your facility, listing your products, having evidence your product is safe (“safety substantiation”), and keeping records of serious adverse reactions. Small businesses get partial exemptions from some of these — not all. Check exactly which apply to you.

    Verify before selling · current MoCRA guidance
  3. 03

    Labels must show five things.

    • Identitywhat it is — e.g. “Eau de Parfum”
    • Net quantityhow much is inside
    • Your businessname and address
    • Ingredientsthe full list
    • Warningsany that apply

    Where each item goes on the package, and exactly how it's worded, has its own rules.

    Verify before selling · FDA labeling guide
  4. 04

    Alcohol perfume is hazmat.

    Alcohol-based perfume is a flammable liquid, which makes it a hazardous material in the mail. With USPS that means ground transport only, specific packaging and markings, and no international shipping. Oil-based perfumes are usually much simpler to post — one more reason to start with oils.

    Verify before selling · USPS Publication 52
  5. 05

    Price it like a business.

    Cost every batch: ingredients, bottle, label, box, marketplace fees — and your time, at an hourly rate you'd actually accept. Then price above that. Never underprice; it's the fastest way to burn out. If shops stock you, wholesale is usually around 50% of retail, so your retail price has to survive being halved.

    materials+bottle+label+time=cost→retail ≥ 2 × wholesale
  6. 06

    Choose where to sell.

    Local markets let people smell before they buy — huge for perfume. Etsy gives you reach, but nobody can smell a photo, so offer sample vials. Your own site keeps the most margin once you have a following. Every platform and market has its own seller rules — read them first.

    Verify before selling · platform policies

Educational guidance, not legal advice. Outside the US the rules are different — in the UK and EU, for example, every cosmetic needs a formal safety assessment before it can be sold.

11

11Troubleshooting

Something smells off?

Every perfumer has made a batch that smells like a hospital or a salad. Find your symptom, then the fix.

It almost certainly hasn't aged yet. Fresh alcohol blends smell sharp and boozy for weeks. Put it back in a dark cupboard for another 4–6 weeks and try again. Also, judge it properly: spray a paper strip or your wrist and wait two minutes — the alcohol flashes off first and the real scent comes through after. If your vodka has a smell of its own, switch to a higher-proof, more neutral alcohol next time.

Your base notes are too weak or missing. Add a fixative — a vanilla bean or a little benzoin — and make sure base notes are really there in your formula. Check your strength, too: a 3% cologne is supposed to fade fast. Next batch, try eau de parfum strength (15–20%), age it the full 4–6 weeks, and apply over moisturised skin.

Water got in — from low-proof vodka, damp botanicals, or a jar that wasn't fully dry. Try chilling the bottle in the fridge for a day, then pouring it through a coffee filter while it's cold; that often clears it. Next time, use the highest-proof alcohol you can get and make sure everything is bone dry. If it's cloudy and smells wrong, that's spoilage — see below.

That's bacteria, mould or rancid oil, and there's no rescuing it. Water-based batches: throw them out. Oil batches that smell of crayons or stale nuts have gone rancid — out as well. Next time, use dry botanicals and clean, dry jars, keep anything with water in the fridge, and label everything with a date so you know how old it is.

Wash it off with mild soap and water, and stop using that blend. Something in it is too concentrated for your skin — cinnamon, clove and citrus are the usual suspects. Dilute more next time, and patch test ingredients one at a time to find the culprit. If the reaction spreads, blisters, or comes with swelling, see a doctor.

That's normal, not a mistake. Your skin's warmth and natural oils change how a perfume unfolds, which is why the same scent smells different on different people. Always judge a blend on skin, over a few hours — never just by sniffing the bottle.

13FAQ

Good questions.

Two answers. On skin: 1–3 hours for most homemade alcohol blends, a bit longer for oils, and longer again on fabric. In the bottle: high-proof alcohol perfume keeps a year or more somewhere cool and dark; oil perfume lasts 6–12 months before it turns rancid; floral water lasts 1–2 weeks in the fridge.

In the US, yes — but you become a cosmetics maker with real responsibilities: product safety, proper labels, possibly MoCRA registration and listing, and hazmat rules for shipping alcohol perfume. Chapter 09 walks through it. Verify the current rules before your first sale.

The warm-oil roll-on in Chapter 02. One bottle of carrier oil, one roll-on bottle, and botanicals you already have — citrus peel, a vanilla bean, dried herbs. No alcohol, no waiting, nothing specialist.

It's maturing. In alcohol, the ingredients slowly react and blend: sharp edges round off, the booziness fades, and new notes appear. That's why the 4–6 week wait matters. If it smells worse — sour, musty — that's spoilage, not maturation.

Yes — it's how most home perfumers graduate from infusions, and it makes a much stronger perfume. Blend them into alcohol or oil using the calculator. Two rules: never put them on skin undiluted, and check each oil's skin limit with your supplier. Some, like cinnamon bark and clove, are very low.

Just concentration. Parfum is 20–30% fragrance concentrate, eau de parfum 15–20%, eau de toilette 5–15%, and cologne 2–4%. Higher usually means stronger and longer-lasting. Exact ranges vary between brands and countries.

You can, but go as high-proof as possible. Standard 80-proof vodka is 60% water, which makes weaker, cloudier infusions. 100-proof is better. Everclear 190 (95% alcohol) or perfumer's alcohol is best, where they're legal to buy.

Isopropyl alcohol is toxic, your skin absorbs it, it dries and irritates, and it smells like a hospital whatever you add to it. Perfume is made with ethanol — the same alcohol that's in drinks.

For alcohol perfume, yes, if you want it to smell finished and last. You can wear it sooner; it just won't be at its best. Oil perfumes and floral waters don't need aging — another reason they're great first projects.

Honestly? Not as strong, and not as long-lasting — commercial perfumes use concentrated natural and synthetic materials designed to last. But homemade can smell beautiful, personal, and unlike anything on a department-store shelf. That's the point.
